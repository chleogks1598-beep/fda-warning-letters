WARNING LETTER
Reference number: 320-27-25

To Dr. Vikas Gupta:

This warning letter advises you of significant violations observed during a U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) inspection of your facility and provides you an opportunity to address them. Promptly address any violations described in this letter, and as part of that effort ensure that you allocate appropriate resources to fully address them and prevent their recurrence. This is not intended to be an all-inclusive list of the violations that may exist at your facility. It is your responsibility to ensure that your firm complies with all requirements of federal law, including FDA regulations. Failure to adequately address any violations may result in regulatory action without further notice.

FDA Inspection

Violations were observed and documented during an inspection of your drug manufacturing facility, Alkem Laboratories Limited, at Srv. No.167, Plot No. 164, 165/1, 165/2, 165/3, 168/215, 168/216, 168/234, Village Dabhel, Daman, Dadra Nagar Haveli & Daman Diu, from April 20 to May 1, 2026. This inspection was conducted under FDA’s statutory authority and public health responsibilities to protect the public from unsafe, ineffective, and poor quality drugs.

This warning letter summarizes significant violations of Current Good Manufacturing Practice (CGMP) regulations for finished pharmaceuticals. See Title 21 Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), parts 210 and 211 (21 CFR parts 210 and 211).

Because your methods, facilities, or controls for manufacturing, processing, packing, or holding do not conform to CGMP, your drug products are adulterated within the meaning of section 501(a)(2)(B) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act), 21 U.S.C. 351(a)(2)(B).

We reviewed your May 22, 2026, response to our Form FDA 483 in detail and acknowledge receipt of your subsequent correspondence.

Violations of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act

The following are violations identified during our inspection and review. As a reminder, this is not an all-inclusive list of violations that may exist at your facility.

1. Your firm failed to thoroughly investigate any unexplained discrepancy or failure of a batch or any of its components to meet any of its specifications, whether or not the batch has already been distributed (21 CFR 211.192).

A. Deficient investigations into unexpected dissolution test results

You failed to adequately investigate unexpected discrepancies. For example, your 2021 feasibility study showed a notable decrease in dissolution rate when excipients from new suppliers were used in the manufacture of (b)(4) tablets, USP. You also observed a notably lower initial dissolution result for (b)(4) tablets, USP, batch #(b)(4), manufactured in July 2022 with excipients from new suppliers. Despite these quality signals, you did not initiate an investigation until you obtained out-of-specification (OOS) dissolution results during release testing in March 2025 for (b)(4) tablets, USP, batches #(b)(4), and out-of-trend (OOT) dissolution results in March 2025 for batch #(b)(4) at the three-month stability interval. Your investigation for these batches concluded “batch to batch inconsistency for drug release and compression characteristics,” and the probable cause as differences in excipient characteristics from new suppliers evaluated in your 2021 feasibility study.

Moreover, FDA tested and obtained failing dissolution results for your (b)(4) tablets, USP, batch #(b)(4). Based on notification of this failure, you then analyzed reserve samples across multiple dosages and identified several batches that did not comply with the dissolution testing specification. You took market action in (b)(4), including recalls, on (b)(4) batches of (b)(4) tablets manufactured from July 2024 to August 2025 for the U.S. market. However, the delay in initiating your investigation resulted in (b)(4) potentially impacted batches that remained on the market through their full (b)(4) shelf life.

In your response, you state batch #(b)(4) did not reach the OOS result threshold until the six-month stability interval in August 2025. You also state you did not conduct an assessment of all material attributes from new component suppliers because it was not required by your procedure, and that procedural gap has since been addressed.

Your response is inadequate. You state that batch #(b)(4) did not fail dissolution until August 2025, but this does not account for the systemic failure to identify and investigate related quality trends across your marketed (b)(4) tablets, USP. Furthermore, your updated procedure for the assessment of component suppliers does not address the retrospective evaluation of prior assessments that failed to evaluate component or finished drug product attributes when components from new suppliers were introduced.

B. Deficient complaint investigations for (b)(4) tablets

You failed to adequately investigate customer complaints regarding (b)(4) tablets. You received approximately 38 complaints for broken and wet tablets distributed to the United States since September 2025. Your investigation indicated the root cause to be mechanical stress during transportation, compounded by excessive headspace inside the bottle. Your corrective action and preventive action (CAPA) included increasing the (b)(4) content of the tablets to provide enhanced protection against breakage and moisture uptake. However, you continued to receive complaints for broken tablets after the CAPAs were implemented, indicating your CAPA may be inadequate. Although you committed to suspend manufacturing of (b)(4) tablets during this inspection, you continue to distribute previously manufactured lots. In addition, you were required to submit a FAR after receiving information about this drug product defect; however, a FAR was not submitted until after the inspection started.

For more information about handling failing, OOS and OOT, or other unexpected results and documentation of your investigations, see FDA’s guidance document Investigating Out-of-Specification (OOS) Test Results for Pharmaceutical Production, at https://www.fda.gov/media/158416/download. While this guidance addresses OOS test results, many of its principles are also relevant to OOT results.

In response to this letter, provide:

  • A retrospective, independent review of all OOS (including in-process and release/stability testing) results for U.S. drug products irrespective of whether the batch was ultimately distributed in the U.S. for the last three years from the initial date of inspection and a report summarizing the findings of the analysis, including the following for each OOS:
    o Determine whether the scientific justification and evidence relating to an invalidated OOS result conclusively or inconclusively demonstrates causative laboratory error.
    o For investigations that conclusively establish laboratory root cause, provide rationale and ensure that all other laboratory methods vulnerable to the same or similar root cause are identified for remediation.
    o For all OOS results found by the retrospective review to have an inconclusive or no root cause identified in the laboratory, include a thorough review of production (e.g., batch manufacturing records, adequacy of the manufacturing steps, suitability of equipment/facilities, variability of raw materials, process capability, deviation history, complaint history, batch failure history). Provide a summary of potential manufacturing root causes for each investigation, any manufacturing operation improvements, and your action plan to address any product quality or patient safety risks for your drug products in U.S. distribution, including potential customer notifications and recalls.
  • A comprehensive review and remediation plan for your OOS and OOT result investigation systems. The CAPA should include but not be limited to addressing the following:
    o Quality unit oversight of laboratory investigations
    o Identification of adverse laboratory control trends
    o Resolution of causes of laboratory variation
    o Initiation of thorough investigations of potential manufacturing causes whenever a laboratory cause cannot be conclusively identified
    o Adequate scoping of each investigation and its CAPA
    o Revised OOS investigation procedures with these and other remediations
    o A similar review and remediation of your system for handling out-of-limit findings (i.e., data that falls outside action limits)
  • A comprehensive, independent assessment of your overall system for investigating deviations, discrepancies, complaints, OOS results, OOT results, and failures. Provide a detailed action plan to remediate this system. Your action plan should include, but not be limited to the following:
    o Staff competencies (including use of appropriate subject matter experts)
    o Written procedures
    o Scientific rigor and foundation
    o Scope determination
    o Root cause evaluations
    o CAPA effectiveness
    o How your firm will ensure all phases of investigations are appropriately conducted
    o And, based on above, detailed systemic improvements to the investigation system
  • An independent assessment and remediation plan for your CAPA program. Provide a report that evaluates whether the program includes effective root cause analysis, ensures CAPA effectiveness, analyzes investigations trends, improves the CAPA program whenever needed, ensures final quality assurance unit decision authority, and is fully supported by executive management.
  • A comprehensive, independent review of your material system, including but not limited to:
    o Evaluating all suppliers of materials (components, containers, and closures) to determine if they are reliable and appropriately qualified.
    o An assessment of all materials to determine whether they are consistently of acceptable quality based on thorough historical review.
    o A review to ensure assigned expiration or retest dates are appropriate (supported by data).
    o Adequacy of the supplier qualification program, and its selection, qualification, and disqualification provisions.
    o Vigilant lifecycle monitoring of all suppliers using sound quality risk management practices.
    o Based on this thorough review, summarize a systemic CAPA that remediates the materials system to ensure ongoing lifecycle oversight of vendor performance and prevents use of unsuitable components (ingredients), containers and closures.

2. Your firm failed to establish adequate written procedures for production and process control designed to assure that the drug products you manufacture have the identity, strength, quality, and purity they purport or are represented to possess (21 CFR 211.100(a)).

The manufacturing process for (b)(4) tablets is not in a state of control. Since 2021, multiple batches of (b)(4) tablets, USP have failed dissolution specifications and were either rejected or recalled.

For example, the tableting (b)(4) has not been thoroughly evaluated to determine the impact on finished drug product dissolution. The (b)(4) specification evolved without clear scientific justification from (b)(4) in your 2016 feasibility study to (b)(4) in an October 2023 batch record.

Your August 2024 investigation cited “higher (b)(4)” as the probable root cause of the dissolution failure and further revised the (b)(4) range to (b)(4). This change in (b)(4) range was also not scientifically justified. Notably, in 2026, you classified (b)(4) as “non-critical” despite being previously identified as the root cause of the dissolution failure.

In addition to (b)(4) tablets, USP, you also provided documentation regarding approximately (b)(4) additional tableted drug product formulations where the (b)(4) ranges were not scientifically justified.

Your firm’s pattern of establishing or changing process parameters without adequate scientific justification indicates a deficient process validation program. Without sufficient oversight of critical process parameter design and implementation, you lack assurance that drug products will consistently and reliably meet your specifications.

In your response, you acknowledge the (b)(4) specifications for (b)(4) tablets, USP were derived from historical trend data rather than science-based studies. You also state your initial feasibility studies prioritized (b)(4) over (b)(4), which was incorporated later only for trend monitoring purposes.

Your response is inadequate. While you state that (b)(4) limits were added through a change control, you provide no evidence that these limits are scientifically justified.

Your firm does not have an adequate ongoing program for monitoring process controls to ensure stable manufacturing operations and consistent drug quality. See FDA’s guidance document Process Validation: General Principles and Practices for general principles and approaches that FDA considers appropriate elements of process validation at https://www.fda.gov/media/71021/download.

In response to this letter, provide:

  • Provide a comprehensive, independent review of your process capability and product quality performance for each of your marketed drug products, including:
    o Comprehensive identification of all defects encountered at each manufacturing process stage.
    o Classification of each identified defect by type, frequency (i.e., average, range), severity, and root cause(s).
    o Analysis of multi-year, longitudinal batch-to-batch trending data for each defect.
    o Analysis of process performance of each critical manufacturing step based on in-process monitoring data.
    o Process capability index calculations for all critical manufacturing steps.
    o Actual in-process and finished drug product yield data for each batch.
    o A thorough independent assessment of both sporadic (i.e., unexpected spikes, shorter term trends) and average variability observed based upon detailed review of above items. Provide analytical findings, associated operational weaknesses and root causes, and potential remediation options.
    o Also, describe the drug product quality data (e.g., defects, complaints) that you monitor in tandem with the process performance feedback to enable a comprehensive ongoing review of state of control.
    o Your commitment to implement CAPA that more effectively implements the above process performance and drug product quality analysis, and to begin conducting annual capability analysis with quality oversight across all drug product lines.
  • A detailed summary of your validation program for ensuring a state of control throughout the drug product lifecycle, along with associated procedures. Describe your program for process performance qualification, and ongoing vigilant monitoring of both intra-batch and inter-batch variation to ensure a continuing state of control. Also, describe your equipment and facility qualification program.
  • A timeline for performing appropriate process performance qualification for each of your marketed drug products.

3. Your firm failed to establish and follow adequate written procedures for cleaning and maintenance of equipment (21 CFR 211.67(b)).

Your firm failed to ensure adequate oversight of your equipment maintenance program. For example, between January 2024 and April 2026, approximately (b)(4) machine parts, including (b)(4) were reported as damaged and removed for destruction, without an adequate system in place to assess the quality and safety impact on drug products manufactured using this unsuitable equipment.

We also note that your (b)(4) system failed to (b)(4) contamination in your distributed drug product, based on a product complaint received in 2022. Although the batch was recalled, your investigation failed to evaluate whether the (b)(4) system could (b)(4) contamination of the complaint sample’s physical dimensions. This gap in your investigation indicates the known deficiency in your (b)(4) controls has not been fully remediated.

Additionally, a (b)(4) dedicated to (b)(4) production, which was cleaned and stored on March 27, 2026, was found with rust-like discoloration on April 20, 2026, demonstrating inadequate post-cleaning inspection and failure to maintain equipment in a suitable state.

In your response, you acknowledge that you updated procedures for categorizing and assessing damaged equipment impact. You also indicated that design deficiencies, such as the lack of reinforced edges on your (b)(4), may accelerate wear and damage to the equipment, and admit that no corrective action was taken to evaluate your (b)(4) system’s capability to reliably (b)(4) with the complaint particle’s size and composition.

Your response is inadequate. You fail to adequately ensure your firm is exercising effective oversight to prevent the use of unsuitable equipment. Furthermore, your response does not provide adequate assurance that your (b)(4) system is adequate to prevent (b)(4)-contaminated drug products from reaching patients.

In response to this letter, provide:

  • Your CAPA plan to implement routine, vigilant operations management oversight of facilities and equipment. This plan should ensure, among other things, prompt detection of equipment/facilities performance issues, effective execution of repairs, adherence to appropriate preventive maintenance schedules, timely technological upgrades to the equipment/facility infrastructure, and improved systems for ongoing senior management review.

Quality Systems

Significant findings in this letter demonstrate that your firm does not operate an effective quality system in accord with CGMP. In addition to the lack of effective management oversight of your production operations, we found your quality unit is not enabled to exercise proper authority and/or has insufficiently implemented its responsibilities. Executive management should immediately and comprehensively assess your company’s global manufacturing operations to ensure that your systems, processes, and drug products conform to FDA requirements.

For guidance on establishing and maintaining CGMP-compliant quality systems, see FDA guidances: Q8(R2) Pharmaceutical Development at https://www.fda.gov/media/71535/download, Q9(R1) Quality Risk Management at https://www.fda.gov/media/167721/download, and Q10 Pharmaceutical Quality System at https://www.fda.gov/media/71553/download.

Drug Production Suspended

We acknowledge your commitment to temporarily suspend production of (b)(4) tablets, strengths (b)(4) mg, (b)(4) mg, and (b)(4) mg (b)(4) count high-density polyethylene (HDPE) bottle pack at this facility for the U.S. market. In response to this letter, clarify whether you intend to resume manufacturing (b)(4) tablets, strengths (b)(4) mg, (b)(4) mg, and (b)(4) mg (b)(4) count HDPE bottle pack for the U.S. market at this facility in the future.

You are responsible for resolving all deficiencies and systemic flaws to ensure your firm is capable of ongoing CGMP compliance. In addition, based upon the nature of the violations we identified at your firm, you should engage a consultant qualified as set forth in 21 CFR 211.34 to assist your firm in meeting CGMP requirements. The qualified consultant should also perform a comprehensive six-system audit of your entire operation for CGMP compliance and evaluate the completion and efficacy of all corrective action and preventive action, before you pursue resolution of your firm’s compliance status with FDA.

Your use of a consultant does not relieve your firm’s obligation to comply with CGMP. Your firm’s executive management remains responsible for resolving all deficiencies and systemic flaws to ensure ongoing CGMP compliance

Conclusion

As previously stated, you are responsible for investigating and determining the root causes of any violations and implementing corrective and preventive measures to ensure future and sustained compliance so that these violations and any others do not occur.

FDA may withhold approval of new applications or supplements listing your firm as a drug manufacturer until any violations are completely addressed and we confirm your compliance with CGMP. We may re-inspect to verify that you have completed corrective actions to any violations.

Failure to address any violations may also result in the FDA refusing admission of articles manufactured at Alkem Laboratories Limited, located at Srv. No.167, Plot No. 164, 165/1, 165/2, 165/3, 168/215, 168/216, 168/234, Village Dabhel, Daman, Dadra Nagar Haveli & Daman Diu – 396210, India, into the United States under section 801(a)(3) of the FD&C Act, 21 U.S.C. 381(a)(3). Articles under this authority that appear to be adulterated may be detained or refused admission, in that the methods and controls used in their manufacture do not appear to conform to CGMP within the meaning of section 501(a)(2)(B) of the FD&C Act, 21 U.S.C. 351(a)(2)(B).

If you are considering an action that is likely to lead to a disruption in the supply of drugs produced at your facility, FDA requests that you contact CDER’s Drug Shortages Staff immediately, at drugshortages@fda.hhs.gov, so that FDA can work with you on the most effective way to bring your operations into compliance with the law. Contacting the Drug Shortages Staff also allows you to meet any obligations you may have to report discontinuances or interruptions in your drug manufacture under 21 U.S.C. 356c(b). This also allows FDA to consider, as soon as possible, what actions, if any, may be needed to avoid shortages and protect the health of patients who depend on your drug products.

Send your written response to CDER-OC-OMQ-Communications@fda.hhs.gov within fifteen business days of receipt of this letter1. If you have information that you believe demonstrates that your products are not in violation of the FD&C Act and FDA regulations, include that information for our consideration.
